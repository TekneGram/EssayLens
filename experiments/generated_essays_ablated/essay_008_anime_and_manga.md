Anime and Manga as Global Culture and Creative Industry

Anime and Manga is very popular around the world. Did you know that manga is sometimes the source for anime stories. If an anime is successful, people want to read the books. But artists feel a lot of pressure.

Anime and manga now function as a useful international business as well as an art form. The *Financial Times* showed that the global anime market reached 3.4 trillion yen in 2023, and more than half of anime revenue came from outside Japan (Financial Times, 2025). That figure showed that anime is part of the global entertainment economy, not a small domestic product. Matt Alt (2025) showed that popular manga series are licensed for animation and then streamed internationally, creating a cycle in which books promote shows and shows promote books. This relationship showed why comics and shows such as *Naruto* and *Demon Slayer* are international. Therefore, anime and manga succeed not only because of artistic appeal but also because they reinforce each other commercially.

Anime and manga also create cultural connections. According to the Anime Tourism Association, its mission is to organize "Japanese Anime 88-Spots" and connect local regions, companies, and content holders in order to welcome tourists and encourage new economic growth (Anime Tourism Association, n.d.). Also, anime can foster connections between people in a local area because people from the local area can sometimes come from different cultures and make Japanese anime more popular around the world. This goal shows how fictional stories can change the local area in a good way. Fans often want to visit the landscapes, shrines, train stations, or neighborhoods where an anime series was based on. In addition, this tourism introduces international visitors to local Japanese communities that might otherwise receive less attention. As a result, anime and manga can act as bridges between media consumption and regional development.

However, the success of anime and manga brings serious costs. Alt (2025) describes the world of *Weekly Shonen Jump* as one of relentless deadlines, intense reader rankings, and heavy physical and mental pressure on creators. He notes that serialized artists can feel trapped in a constant cycle of "next, next, next," and that health concerns have pushed the industry to create more support systems. This evidence suggests that global demand may reward speed and dependable franchises more than experimentation. This means that maybe the original idea of anime is getting lost. Like some people can't create new things. In the same article, Alt argues that reader surveys and franchise logic can encourage sameness in content, even while they produce major hits. However effective this system may be in business terms, it risks exhausting artists and narrowing the range of stories that reach audiences.

Anime and manga are valuable precisely because they combine artistic imagination with global influence, but that strength should not be measured by profits alone. Their growth has expanded Japan's cultural presence, supported tourism, and built a strong media ecosystem through the connection between printed and animated storytelling. At the same time, the industry depends on demanding labor and increasingly commercial formulas. Therefore, the future of anime and manga will be strongest if the industry balances worldwide expansion with healthier working conditions and greater room for creative risk.
