Music Festivals Need More Than Good Lineups

Music festivals are often very exciting for many people around the world. However, they are also quite important. Festivals make local culture more interesting. At the same time, recent reporting shows that festivals also cause waste, emissions, and financial pressure for organizers and host communities. However, this essay will argue that music festivals are essential for the community.

Music festivals are important because they help build community. In New Zealand, Henry Oliver (2026) describes festivals as a cultural rite of passage. He notes that they are also launch pads for new artists. That role matters because festivals do not only show famous performers; they also give small acts a chance to reach a big audience. Festivals bring together different people together even if they have different ages, neighborhoods, or tastes. Even when the music is the main thing, the event can still create a feeling of being together with new people. So, the cultural value of festivals is not only in entertainment but also in the networks and opportunities they create.

Music festivals also make economic benefits for host cities. Treefort Music Fest 2025 supported 128799 jobs. It also made about $4.7 million in labor income, and made roughly $15.8 million across Idaho. The report said it made about $1.4 million in taxes. These figures show that money from festivalds also comes from hotels, restaurants, and transport. Festivals get attention from leaders who want to make tourism stronger. So, the argument is stronger when the benefits can reach local workers and businesses. So, festivals that make both the music scene and also the economy stronger has stronger public support.

Music festivals face many problems. Leah Thomas (2023) shows that even a well-known festival such as Coachella needs many systems for trash, and reducing plastic pollution needs policy changes. In addition, Jim Ottewill (2026) reports that festivals discussed in the UK Parliament generate 25,800 tonnes of waste, 22,876 tonnes of CO2, and use 185 million litres of water every year. The same article offers evidence that improvement is possible: Massive Attack's 2024 Act 1.5 event reduced energy-related greenhouse gas emissions by 98 percent and made an 89 percent recycling rate (Ottewill, 2026). Money is also part of the problem, since Oliver (2026) notes that many independent festivals are struggling to survive. So, festivals should be judged not only by their lineups but also by how seriously organizers address waste, transport, and long-term viability.

In conclusion, I think people should go to music festivals. That's because music festivals can bring so much fun. Don't worry about the pollution, just clean it up!
