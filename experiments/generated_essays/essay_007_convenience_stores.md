Convenience Stores and Everyday Food Access

Bright lights, long hours, and quick transactions have made convenience stores a familiar part of modern neighborhoods. These stores are often criticized for selling expensive snacks, sugary drinks, and other low-nutrition items. However, they also fill a practical gap for people who need food close to home, especially in places where full supermarkets are harder to reach. In many communities, the nearest convenience store is a routine source of groceries, drinks, and basic household goods. Convenience stores remain important in urban and low-access neighborhoods because they save time, support local food access, and can serve community needs, even though their limited healthy options prevent them from replacing supermarkets.

Convenience stores play an important role because they are physically close to where people live and travel. The U.S. Department of Agriculture's Economic Research Service explains that its Food Access Research Atlas measures supermarket accessibility and low-income, low-access census tracts across the United States (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Economic Research Service [USDA ERS], 2025). This broader concern about distance helps explain why small neighborhood stores remain significant. According to an Associated Press report, about 14% of SNAP benefits in 2024 were spent at smaller grocery and convenience stores, which are often easier for beneficiaries to reach than large superstores (Durbin, 2025). Therefore, convenience stores reduce the time and transportation barriers that can make regular shopping difficult for working families, older adults, and residents of food deserts.

Convenience stores also matter because they can strengthen neighborhood economies and offer practical services beyond snacks. Durbin (2025) reports that small grocers and convenience stores felt an immediate sales decline when SNAP benefits were delayed, showing that these stores are closely tied to local household spending. The same article describes Kansas City convenience stores that stocked produce through a nonprofit partnership, suggesting that some stores are adapting to community needs instead of relying only on junk food sales. In addition, small stores often stay open later than supermarkets and provide quick access to milk, bread, fruit, or prepared food during irregular work hours. For example, a late-night shift worker may depend on a nearby convenience store when larger retailers are closed. These functions help explain why such stores remain woven into daily neighborhood life.

However, convenience stores have serious limitations that should not be ignored. Research on rural food environments found that grocery stores scored much higher than convenience stores on measures of healthy food availability, with grocery stores averaging far stronger nutrition environment scores than convenience stores (McCormack et al., 2021). This finding supports a common criticism: convenience stores may offer access, but access alone does not guarantee healthy choices. When shelves are dominated by processed foods, customers may face a limited range of affordable nutritious items. As a result, communities that depend too heavily on convenience stores may still experience poor diet quality. Convenience stores can help reduce distance barriers, yet they cannot fully solve the larger structural problem of unequal access to full-service grocery stores.

Convenience stores remain important because they make food and basic goods easier to reach, support neighborhood spending, and provide flexible service when larger stores are unavailable. At the same time, their weak record on healthy food variety shows that convenience should not be confused with complete food security. A practical response is not to dismiss convenience stores, but to improve them while also expanding access to supermarkets and fresh food programs. If communities treat these stores as partners rather than perfect solutions, convenience stores can become a stronger part of a healthier local food system.
