AI in Education: A Tool That Needs Human Guidance

Artificial intelligence has entered education with unusual speed. In only a few years, teachers, schools, and students have moved from curiosity about chatbots to daily debates about lesson planning, tutoring, cheating, and classroom work. This change has encouraged both optimism and fear. Some educators see AI as a way to personalize learning and reduce repetitive tasks, while others worry that it could weaken thinking and deepen inequality. AI should be integrated into education as a supervised support tool because it can improve efficiency and access to help, but it must remain under clear human guidance to protect learning quality, academic integrity, and equity.

One important reason to include AI in education is that it can reduce teacher workload and expand academic support. Gallup found that 6 in 10 U.S. public K-12 teachers used AI tools during the 2024-25 school year, and regular users estimated saving 5.9 hours per week, or about six weeks across a school year (Malek Ash, 2025). That time matters because teachers often spend many hours creating materials, adjusting lessons, and handling administrative work. If AI helps draft worksheets or modify materials for different student needs, teachers may spend more energy on feedback and direct instruction. UNESCO also notes that generative AI may support curriculum design, teaching, and learning when its use is carefully validated (Miao & Holmes, 2023). Therefore, AI can serve a productive role when it supports the teacher rather than replacing the teacher.

A second reason for caution is that AI can interfere with genuine learning when schools use it without clear limits. Lee (2025) argues that AI has changed the method of cheating more than the amount of cheating, which is an important distinction. In data from public and charter high schools, 11% of students in 2024 and 15% in 2025 reported using AI to complete an entire assignment, while larger numbers used it to generate ideas or revise work (Lee, 2025). These findings show that student use is not all the same. However, they also show that schools cannot ignore the problem. When students rely on AI to do the intellectual work for them, writing, analysis, and problem solving may become weaker. UNESCO similarly warns that the lack of regulation leaves institutions unprepared and users insufficiently protected (Miao & Holmes, 2023). Responsible use therefore requires more than excitement about innovation.

Clear policy and training are the conditions that can make AI educationally useful instead of harmful. The AI Education Project's aiEDU Pulse found that more than 80% of educators wanted AI-related professional development, while 75% supported teaching students about AI itself (AI Education Project, 2024). At the same time, 56.9% believed AI was more likely to widen achievement gaps than narrow them (AI Education Project, 2024). These results suggest that educators do not simply want more technology; they want better guidance. UNESCO's guidance supports this approach by calling for privacy protection, ethical validation, and age-appropriate policy frameworks (Miao & Holmes, 2023). AI literacy can also help students understand when AI is a useful assistant and when it becomes a substitute for thought.

AI should be used in education as a guided support tool, not as a replacement for teachers or student thinking. It can save time, help prepare materials, and offer added support, but it also raises serious concerns about cheating, overreliance, privacy, and inequality. The strongest response is neither total rejection nor blind acceptance. Schools should adopt AI carefully, train teachers and students to use it responsibly, and keep human judgment at the center of learning.
