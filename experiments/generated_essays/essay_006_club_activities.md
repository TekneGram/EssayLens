Club Activities: Valuable When They Build Connection

Some of the most important lessons in school happen after the last class ends. Club activities may look secondary compared with formal lessons, yet they often shape how students feel about school and about themselves. Debate about extracurricular life is sometimes too simple, because supporters often act as if more activities always lead to better students. Research suggests a more careful conclusion. Although club activities do not automatically raise academic performance, they should be encouraged because they often strengthen belonging, mental well-being, and school persistence when schools prevent overscheduling.

Club activities can give students a sense of belonging that ordinary classes do not always create. In general, extracurricular activities are organized school-related experiences outside the formal curriculum and often include clubs, arts, and sports (Wikipedia contributors, 2025). This part of school life matters because it gives students a shared purpose and a smaller community within a large institution. For some students, that connection may be especially important. Lemkin et al. (2018) found that among maltreated youth, participation in school clubs was associated with 2.54 times the odds of graduating. This finding does not mean every club guarantees success. However, it does suggest that school clubs can strengthen attachment to school, especially for students who might otherwise drift away from it.

In addition, the strongest benefits of club activities appear to be social and emotional rather than purely academic. Many people assume that joining several activities will naturally improve grades, but the evidence is more limited than that belief suggests. O'Flaherty et al. (2022) examined extracurricular participation with a fixed-effects approach designed to reduce hidden bias. They found that the positive effects became smaller after stricter analysis, and academic effects were minimal. Even so, sports still showed beneficial effects for mental health, while arts participation remained linked to better peer-group characteristics. Therefore, club activities should not be defended mainly as an academic shortcut. Their stronger value lies in helping students develop confidence, healthier peer environments, and a more positive school experience.

However, club activities are not beneficial in every form, because too many commitments can create new problems. Owens and Weiss (2017) identified extracurricular activities as one factor that can contribute to insufficient sleep in adolescents, along with heavy homework and early school start times. Lack of sleep can harm attention, mood, and school performance, which weakens the very benefits clubs are supposed to support. Furthermore, the kind of activity also matters. Fagan et al. (2022) found that non-competitive school sport reduced some substance-use risks, but other forms of sport showed mixed patterns, and school connectedness shaped the results. For this reason, schools should not judge success by the number of activities alone. Instead, they should focus on whether clubs are meaningful, balanced, and connected to student well-being.

Club activities deserve support, but not because they magically improve every area of student life. Their greatest strength is that they help students feel connected, supported, and motivated to remain engaged with school. At the same time, the research shows that academic gains are limited and that overscheduling can reduce the benefits. Therefore, schools should expand access to meaningful club opportunities while also protecting students from excessive pressure. When participation is balanced and purposeful, club activities become an important part of education rather than just an added burden.
